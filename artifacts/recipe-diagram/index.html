How the format works
  • Indent an ingredient under the step that eats it. That's the whole idea. Any line with lines indented beneath it is an operation; any line without children is an ingredient.
  • Ingredients are US | metric | name | note. The note is optional; so is the metric. An amount with no number (pinch, to taste) is left alone when scaling.
  • Operations are verb | detail — bake | {oven}, 30 to 40 min. The verb is what shows in the box.
  • Operations nest, and they can branch: two whisked mixtures meeting in one bowl is just two operations indented under a third. (See Buttermilk Pancakes.)
  • Header lines at zero indent: title: yield: pan: oven: prep: finish:. {pan} and {oven} interpolate — change the pan and the instructions rewrite themselves.
  • Name a branch with @name after the verb — whisk @dry. The name shows on the box and lets the written recipe say “the dry and wet mixtures” instead of “the two mixtures”.
  • Use an ingredient twice with a line starting >, written like any ingredient but naming one you already declared: > 2 Tbs | 28 g | unsalted butter. The declaration keeps the full amount and picks up a “held back” note; the reference is where the held-back part goes. (See Skillet Cornbread.)
  • Don't want to type it out? “Paste a recipe” converts an ordinary one, and the same dialog has a prompt you can hand to any AI assistant — paste its answer back in the same box and it gets used as written.
  • Times in a detail are read for the kitchen timer: 30 to 40 min, 1 hr 30 min, 45 sec, 1 to 2 hours. A phrase starting until… is kept as the doneness cue, and overnight is understood as a rest rather than something to stand and watch.

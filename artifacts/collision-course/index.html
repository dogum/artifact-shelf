How this works

Every point of light here is a star, and every one of them is being moved by your graphics card, many times a second.

Right now

The method

Each galaxy is a dark-matter halo, modeled as a softened point mass (a Plummer sphere), carrying a disk and bulge of stars. The halos pull on each other. The stars feel every halo but have no mass of their own. This is the restricted many-body approach that Alar and Juri Toomre used in 1972 to show that the bridges and tails of peculiar galaxies are tides, not explosions or magnetism.

Halos that overlap lose orbital energy to dynamical friction, modeled here as a drag that switches on as they interpenetrate. That is what lets two galaxies fall back together and merge instead of passing like ships in the night.

The GPU advances every star with a leapfrog integrator through WebGL2 transform feedback. Halo orbits, the Sun, and the event forecast on the timeline run on the CPU with the same equations.

The Local Group scene

This one starts from today’s measured positions, velocities and masses of the Milky Way, Andromeda, M33 and the Large Magellanic Cloud, the same inputs Sawala et al. (2025) used. Each possible future draws those inputs from their measurement errors. As in the paper, halos are softened at 20 kpc, feel Chandrasekhar dynamical friction inside their virial radius, satellites are absorbed once they come within 20 kpc, and the Milky Way and Andromeda count as merged at the same distance. Run that way, this toy lands within a few points of the published odds.

The Sun in this scene starts where the Sun really is: 8.1 kpc from the galactic center, moving at 246 km/s.

What it leaves out

  • Gas, dust and star formation. Real collisions light up with new stars; these don't.
  • The stars' own gravity, so there are no bars, and the initial spiral arms wind up instead of persisting.
  • Realistic halo shapes. Point-like halos make tails longer and let more stars escape than in nature.

Scales are approximate: one simulation unit is 10 kiloparsecs (about 33,000 light-years) and 45 million years.

Sources

  • Lynds & Toomre (1976), On the interpretation of ring galaxies, ApJ 209, 382.
  • van der Marel et al. (2012), The M31 Velocity Vector III: first passage in about 4 billion years, merger at 5.9, and an 85% chance the Sun ends up farther out.
  • Sawala et al. (2025), Apocalypse When? No Certainty of a Milky Way–Andromeda Collision, Nature Astronomy: a merger within 10 billion years in about half of all futures, two thirds without the LMC, one third without M33. Summary by UWA.
